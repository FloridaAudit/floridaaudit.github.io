Florida Audit Project

Florida has had the same party in control of the governorship, the House and the Senate since 1999. That is twenty-eight years of unified control, which means twenty-eight years of results that can be measured against what they cost.

This project publishes charts of Florida public data, one number at a time, with the source on the chart. Nine areas: taxes, healthcare, education, roads and transit, insurance, public investment, housing, and how the state is governed.

It is not a campaign and it endorses nobody. Where the numbers are good, they are published the same as where they are bad. Florida has the lowest public university tuition in the country and the highest average auto insurance cost in the country. Both are here.

How to check any number on any chart

Every chart carries its sources, numbered, with a direct link to the original document or table. The data behind each chart is in this repository as a CSV, in the folder for that chart, along with a note on how it was assembled and anything that was excluded.

If a chart says something you don't believe, the intended response is that you go pull the source and check it. That is the whole design.

Sources

Almost everything here comes from government or official statistical sources: the Florida Office of Economic and Demographic Research, the Florida Division of Elections, the Florida Department of Revenue, the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation, the Revenue Estimating Conference, the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey, NCES, NAEP, NHTSA, the Federal Transit Administration, and the NAIC.

Where a non-governmental source is used, it is named on the chart. Two examples: the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy for tax incidence by income group, and the National Education Association for teacher salary rankings. Both publish their methodology, both are linked, and both are the standard source for that particular measure. Use them with that in mind.

Rules this project follows

Real dollars. Any dollar amount compared across years is inflation-adjusted, and the base year is stated. Where a figure is in current dollars it says so on the chart.

Whole series, not chosen endpoints. Charts show the full available period rather than the two years that make the strongest point. Where a series breaks, the break is shown rather than smoothed over.

Stated vintages. Every figure carries its year. Where two figures on the same chart come from different years, the chart says so.

Like-for-like ratios. No percentage or ratio where the numerator and the denominator come from different years, unless both are put on the same basis and that basis is stated on the chart.

Axes that include zero. A chart whose value axis excludes zero makes every movement on it look larger than it is. Bar and line charts here start at zero. Where a chart legitimately cannot, as when comparing a narrow band across counties, the axis is drawn with labelled ticks so the range is visible rather than assumed. Diverging charts, which put losses and gains either side of a real zero, are not truncations.

No adjectives. The headline of each chart is a factual sentence containing the number. Whether that number is good or bad is left to the reader.

One known gap. The Bureau of Labor Statistics never published a Consumer Price Index for October 2025, because of the lapse in appropriations. The 2025 annual average used here is therefore the mean of eleven months, 321.943, and two charts deflate to 2025 dollars using it. Recomputing with a different assumption about the missing month gives a slightly different real figure.

Corrections

Errors get fixed in public. A corrected chart is republished with a note saying what was wrong, what it now says, and the date. Old versions stay in the repository's history rather than disappearing.

To report an error: [EMAIL]. You do not need to be polite about it.

Reuse

Everything here is released into the public domain under CC0. Use it, crop it, re-typeset it, translate it, put it on a mailer, rebuild it from the CSVs and make it better. No attribution required and no permission needed. If you are a newsroom and want the underlying data in a different shape, ask.

Who runs it

One person, anonymously, on their own time, with no funding and no affiliation to any campaign, committee, party or organization.

The reason for anonymity is straightforward: the arguments here should be checked against the sources, not against whoever is making them. Everything on every chart is verifiable without knowing anything about the author. If the numbers are right they are right regardless, and if they are wrong, knowing the author's name would not have helped.
