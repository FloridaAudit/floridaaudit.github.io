Florida Audit Project

One party has held the governorship, the House and the Senate in Florida since 1999. Twenty-eight years of unified control is twenty-eight years of results that can be measured against what they cost.

This project charts Florida public data, with the source on the chart: taxes, housing and wages, insurance, schools, health coverage, roads and transit, public investment, and how the state is governed. 

How to check any number

Every chart carries numbered sources linking to the original document, and the data behind it is published here as a CSV with a note on how it was assembled and what was excluded. If a chart says something you do not believe, go and pull the source. If you disagree with the data, submit a correction to thefloridaaudit@proton.me.

Almost all the data comes from government statistics: Florida's Office of Economic and Demographic Research, Division of Elections, Department of Revenue, Office of Insurance Regulation and Revenue Estimating Conference, and federally the Census Bureau, NCES, NAEP, NHTSA, the Federal Transit Administration and the NAIC. Any non-government data sources are marked on the chart (as with ITEP for tax incidence and the NEA for teacher pay).

What is covered

Affordability and public services, not everything the state does. Time windows differ because each chart uses the full period its source publishes on one basis, from 1998 for the legislative record to 2010 for rents. Ranks are out of the 50 states and D.C. Direct comparisons use Texas, Georgia, North Carolina and Arizona for size and growth, Virginia, California and Massachusetts for policy contrast; another set would rank differently.

Rules this project follows

Real dollars. Dollars compared across years are inflation-adjusted and the base year is stated. Current-dollar figures say so.

Whole series, not chosen endpoints. Charts show the full available period, and a break in a series is shown rather than smoothed.

Stated vintages. Every figure carries its year, and a chart mixing years says so.

Like-for-like ratios. No ratio whose top and bottom come from different years unless both are put on one basis and that basis is stated.

Axes include zero. Where a chart legitimately cannot, it draws labelled ticks so the range stays visible.

No adjectives. The headline is a factual sentence containing the number. Good or bad is the reader's call.

One known gap. The Bureau of Labor Statistics never published a Consumer Price Index for October 2025, because of the lapse in appropriations, so the 2025 annual average used here is the mean of eleven months, 321.943.

Corrections

Errors get fixed in public. A corrected chart keeps its URL, carries a dated note saying what was wrong and what it now says, and its old version stays in this repository's public history. To report one: thefloridaaudit@proton.me. 

Reuse

Public domain, CC0. Crop it, re-typeset it, translate it, rebuild it from the CSVs and make it better. Newsrooms wanting the data in another shape can ask. No attribution required.

Who runs it

Independently run and anonymous, with no funding and no affiliation to any campaign, committee, party or organization.

Computational tools and AI are used as data-engineering assistants, to write extraction scripts, calculate rates and check tables against each other. Every dataset, citation and sentence is verified by hand against the original public record, and nothing is published without that confirmation.

The arguments here should be checked against the sources, not against whoever is making them.
